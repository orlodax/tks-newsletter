010 · Chapter I

The 569 issues

A clean block at kick-off, weeks of quiet, then bursts, a day at a time. Four projects, the same curve.

The problem in one sentence

The typical customer doesn’t have a clear picture at the start: they discover what they want by looking at what we deliver. That is natural, and it isn’t the problem. The problem is that today we are the ones paying for that discovery: the scope grows, the price and the date don’t, and the hours and tokens other projects need end up there.

The remedy is not to stop the customer from discovering. It is to make sure every expansion has a price and an explicit decision before it turns into work.

In August, in issue 007, we separated churn (a requirement that replaces another) from creep (a requirement added while nothing leaves), and wrote that creep is the only one of the two a written rule can prevent. That was the picture from the industry literature. On November 4 we counted our own: the 569 issues in the repositories of four Odoo projects, read from the GitHub API together with sub-issues, types, labels and status on the board. The customers stay anonymous here: they are projects A, B, C and D.

The same curve, in four projects

The shape is the same everywhere: a clean block at kick-off, weeks of quiet, then bursts packed into a few days. Project A looks like the exception and isn’t: it is just the youngest. On October 22 it took 16 issues in one day, from a functional analysis that the customer’s consultant had rewritten. It is project B’s curve, two months behind.

Six numbers that carry weight

  • 41issues opened on project B on October 20, in a single day. 21 are bugs, and 13 cite our on-screen acceptance test.
  • 52 / 103of the closed issues on project B lasted two days or less: work found and fixed in the same round, but paid for at the full price of an issue, with board, brief and review.
  • 40 / 60of project B’s bugs were found by our own review or the tester, not by the customer. On project C, 20 of 37.
  • 16 / 16items in Awaiting Quote across the whole board: all from project C, all from October 8. Project B, with 48 features opened after kick-off, has none.
  • 40decision issues on project B, each with context, options and references to the code: analysis done, and paid for, before the customer chooses.
  • 88 / 152flat issues, with no epic, on project C: the epic structure broke down halfway through the project.

Where creep comes in

We open the issues ourselves, through the agents, in 70–100% of cases. But the source they cite in the body tells where the extra work comes from, and there are three channels, each with its own remedy.

Channel 1 · the customer, in bursts

Register mails, feedback meetings, rewritten analyses

On project B, 38 issues cite a meeting and 22 a mail; on project C, 62 cite feedback. The typical shape is an epic called “Feedback July 20” with 18 children, or the customer’s register with its R-numbered entries, which turn into 19–21 issues a day. Six days account for 70% of project B.

It comes in batches, on known days. It doesn’t need a continuous rule: it needs a ritual for those days.

Channel 2 · ourselves

Defects found by the pipeline, each with its own issue

The on-screen acceptance test and the tester work: they find things. But today every finding becomes an issue with an epic, a 1,400-character body, board and sprint, even when it closes two days later. The engine of this channel is a rule of ours: “found work ⇒ open the issue now”.

It is continuous, and it is an effect of the pipeline. It gets fixed in the agents’ rules, not in the contract.

Channel 3 · the disguised defect

Features dressed as bugs, in the same batch

In project C’s mails of October 22, real bugs, such as a salesperson being able to confirm an order with approvals still pending, sit next to pure features: management dashboards, daily to-dos on the Top 50, a Word export of a template. They get the same treatment. The gate exists — it is the Awaiting Quote status — but only one project uses it.

There is no mechanical test that says “bug” or “feature”. Until there is one, everything is in.

Three channels, three remedies. The third lacks a criterion, and rule A supplies it; B and C close the first; the second is ours, and D corrects it.

010 · Chapter II

Four rules

One in the quote, one in how issues are born, one in the mail, one in the agents’ rules.

A

A. The quote defines the MVP as a list of demonstrable paths

A kick-off scope already exists today, but it is written by module: “onboarding module, 2-step flow”. When “renewing from the portal saves the card even without auto-renewal” comes in, nobody can say whether it is in or out. So it is in.

The form that solves this is already in our pipeline: it is the path the on-screen acceptance test walks, and the demo that comes out of it. The MVP in the quote becomes a list of ten to twenty sentences such as “the member renews from the portal with a saved card” or “the office imports the contact records from Excel”. Delivering the MVP means the demo of those paths, on a date, plus the customer’s signature.

From then on the definition of a bug is mechanical: a bug breaks a demonstrated path. Everything else is a feature, and a feature after the signature is a quote. We give the customer a list of sentences to sign, not an analysis document they don’t know how to write.

B

B. After the signature, every new issue is born in Awaiting Quote

Today a new issue is born Ready, by inertia. With the MVP signed, the default flips: anything that is born and isn’t a sub-issue of an MVP epic lands in Awaiting Quote on its own, and it is whoever writes the quote who takes it out. The burden moves to whoever wants it worked on.

In practice it is a board automation, plus the devkit hook that already refuses a brief when it cites a decision issue or an issue in Awaiting Quote. It asks no judgment of an agent mid-run, which is the weak spot of any “check the scope”.

C

C. Mails and meetings produce a three-stamp register, not issues

A mail or a meeting doesn’t generate issues. It generates a register, written by whoever handles the mail, person or agent, in which every request gets one of three stamps:

  • BugBreaks a demonstrated path. Becomes a Ready issue that cites the path.
  • DecisionThe customer has to choose. A short issue: the question and the options in ten lines.
  • Out of scopeOne line in the register, “to be quoted”. No issue until it is quoted.

The reply to the customer is the register itself. Project C already has one, with its R-numbered entries: today we receive it and turn it into 21 issues a day. With the stamps, the customer sees that R102 is “to be quoted”, and stops expecting it in the next release.

The control has to live in the format, not in a checklist run by hand. On the day 41 requests arrive, nobody runs a checklist: there must be no way to transcribe a mail that doesn’t go through the three stamps. The mail pass in devkit already exists, and it needs this classification added.

The same register is also the answer to a subscription model, if we ever move to one: the measure of how much the customer asks and how much we work is that file, one line per request, with stamp, estimate and status.

D

D. Devkit rules for the agents: finding is not opening

The rule “found work ⇒ open the issue now” has to be rewritten on three levels:

  1. Found inside the scope of the issue in progress: it gets fixed in the fix round, with no issue. The review finds, the builder fixes, done.
  2. Found outside the issue but inside an MVP path: a single “leftovers” issue per wave, with a checklist, closed in one go. Not twenty issues with twenty bodies.
  3. Found outside the MVP paths: an issue, yes, but born in Awaiting Quote (rule B), and no builder receives it until it is quoted.

And decision issues go on a diet: the question and the options in ten lines, the analysis after the answer. If an option implies new development, the issue isn’t a decision: it is a quote.

010 · Chapter III

Before and after

What happens before the MVP, what to do with the issues already open, and what is decided.

The pre-MVP: paid discovery, not Odoo left to the customer

The idea on the morning of November 4 was this: install Odoo with no development, let the customer use it in their own flows, and have them tell us what they really want. On paper it is right. The data casts doubt on it, for a precise reason.

Project A had a functional analysis written by a consultant, and on October 22 the analysis was rewritten anyway. Customers don’t discover what they want by looking at an empty Odoo: they discover it by looking at their own nearly finished version. Odoo with no development, for a nonprofit association or a field service company, has two possible outcomes: the customer doesn’t use it, or it produces the same flood of “it should do X” that we want to avoid, only earlier.

The version that holds up is a paid, time-boxed discovery phase:

  • an install with no development, plus two or three configuration sessions done together with the customer and led by us;
  • a small fixed price and a fixed duration;
  • as the deliverable, the list of MVP paths (rule A) and the quote for the MVP.

Only one thing changes from the original idea: we don’t wait for the customer to figure it out alone, we guide them, and that time is billed. It is the fixed-price discovery, separated from the build, that issue 007 listed fourth in its survey. To be offered to whoever arrives without an analysis; whoever has one goes straight to the list of paths.

The issues already open

The same rules, applied backwards, say what to do with the 233 issues open across the four projects:

  1. Set a retroactive scope for each project: the kick-off epics; for project C, the R codes of the analysis.
  2. Re-stamp the open issues: those inside the scope stay where they are, those outside go to Awaiting Quote, decisions shrink to the question and the options.
  3. Group the leftovers: the small issues inside the scope go into one “leftovers” issue per project, closed in one go.

It is agent work, with a script and a human confirmation pass. And we already have the “before” to measure whether it worked: the numbers in this note.

The decisions

As of November 4, of the four points two are decided and two remain open. Rule D has no row here: it concerns only our agents, and it goes into devkit.
