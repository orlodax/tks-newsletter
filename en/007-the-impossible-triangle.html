Editorial

The Impossible Triangle

Why fixed price, fixed timeline, and ever-changing requirements cannot coexist — and what organizations actually do once they stop pretending otherwise.

Sections 01–06 · diagnosis   |   Sections 07–13 · operating plan
Established
01Base mechanics

The Iron Triangle

Every project has three variables: scope, time, cost. You get to fix two. The third stays free — always, whether anyone admits it or not.

A contract that pretends to fix all three does not remove the free variable: it pushes it underground. It resurfaces as unpaid overtime, as quality silently eroded, or as permanent tension between whoever sold the project and whoever builds it.

02Differential diagnosis

Two Problems, Not One

“Requirements always change” hides two distinct phenomena with distinct remedies. Treating them with the same instrument is the most common reason agile adoption fails to reduce budget overrun.

Churn

Requirement A is replaced by A′. Same size, different priority. The estimated total is unchanged.

Absorbed by iteration: sprints, backlog, replanning.

Creep

A′ is added to A, with nothing removed. The estimated total grows; the contract does not.

Not absorbed: governed, through explicit scope-for-scope trade.

It is creep, not churn, that burns budgets. And it is the only one of the two entirely preventable with a written rule.

03Estimation

The Cone Does Not Narrow With Time

Early estimates are systematically imprecise — not from incompetence, but because of the statistical shape of the unknown at that stage. The Cone of Uncertainty (McConnell) quantifies how much.

Error factor by stage
StageRange
Initial concept0.25x – 4x
Approved product definition0.5x – 2x
Requirements complete0.67x – 1.5x
UI design complete0.8x – 1.25x
Detailed design complete0.9x – 1.15x

The point that matters: the cone narrows with decisions made — requirements locked, design settled — not with weeks elapsed. If churn and creep stay endemic, the cone stays wide for the project's entire duration, not just at the start.

Secondary effect · anchoring

The moment a point number is said out loud — even as “just a ballpark” — it fixes in the listener's mind and becomes nearly impossible to move, regardless of the disclaimers around it. The damage happens when it is spoken, not when it is signed.

04Framing

Known-Unknown, Unknown-Unknown

Not every problem can be estimated the same way, because they do not live in the same kind of domain. The Cynefin framework (Dave Snowden, 1999) distinguishes four decision-making contexts, each with a different appropriate response.

The Complicated domain is where known-unknowns live: we do not have the answer yet, but we know what we do not know, and expert analysis alone can close it. The Complex domain is where unknown-unknowns live: we do not even know what we do not know, and no amount of desk analysis will surface it. It must be discovered in the field, through experiments.

Software development with evolving requirements lives almost entirely in Complex. That is the technical reason — not a methodology preference — why a detailed upfront plan, however well made, cannot be sufficient on its own.

05Industry landscape

Five Mechanisms, by Cost/Leverage

What the industry uses, from best cost-to-leverage ratio to most burdensome. This is the landscape; the plan we recommend starts at section 07.

1

Pure agile sprint — floating scope, fixed time and cost

Sprint length and team cost locked; scope is the only thing that moves, via backlog reprioritization. This is the Agile Manifesto's design goal, not a workaround.

2

Scope-for-scope trade

Zero cost, pure discipline: nothing enters without something of equivalent size leaving, or dropping to the back of the reprioritized backlog.

3

Formal Change Request with written impact estimate

Every new or changed item gets a time/cost delta quoted before acceptance; the client signs off on the delta. Works under any contract type, fixed price included.

4

T&M with a spending cap, or fixed-price discovery separated from build

Converts unknown-unknowns into known-unknowns before quoting the build phase. Useful when the client structurally needs a fixed price for their own internal budget approval.

5

Target-cost / gain-share

Maximum incentive alignment, maximum contractual overhead. Well established in construction; in software it is real but a minority practice. Do not assume the client's legal team has ever seen one.

06Hidden risk

The Shared-Portfolio Trap

When several fixed-price contracts share the same team, the problem stops being per-project. If no contract carries its own margin to absorb churn and creep, the only available buffer becomes the shared pool of people.

The result: capacity is pulled from one client to save another client's deadline. The operation is invisible until someone measures it — and it poisons every future quote, because the “historical” timings used to estimate already contain hidden overtime and cross-client cannibalization.

From Diagnosis to Plan: Five Moves

The preceding sections describe why the problem exists. These describe what to do, with quantities, formulas, and usable contract language.

Each move is designed to apply upstream, to every contract, not case by case in flight. Taken together, they move the absorption margin inside the individual contract — and that, not an internal policy, is what defuses the portfolio trap from section 06.

08Move 01 · against creep

Swap, Don't Stack

Jeff Sutherland's Changes for Free clause turns the scope-for-scope trade from an informal favour by the team into a contractual right for the client. The client gets real flexibility; the supplier gets a total that does not grow. Neither party loses anything they actually had.

Draft clause · to be adapted with counsel At the start of each sprint the Client may replace any not-yet-started backlog item with one or more items of collectively equivalent or lesser estimated size, at no additional cost and with no change to the delivery date. Introducing items that increase the estimated total constitutes a Change Request under clause [X] and triggers renegotiation of terms and consideration.

The three conditions that make it enforceable

Condition Why
Backlog fully estimated before signature Without comparable sizes, “equivalent” is not verifiable
Not-yet-started items only Work already done is sunk cost, not tradeable
Trade window at sprint start only Mid-sprint trading reintroduces exactly the churn it was meant to remove
Money for Nothing

Sutherland's twin clause — the client may terminate early paying a fraction (typically 20%) of remaining contract value, once they already have enough value — is documented but far less adopted.

Inferred: in a single-client context, leading with it risks reading as disinterest in the project. It works as a second move, not a first.

09Move 02 · the margin

Calculated Buffer, Not Guessed

A “let's add 15%” is always negotiable downward, because it has no basis. A number derived from data survives the negotiation. Three-point estimation (PERT) supplies both required quantities: the expected value and its dispersion.

Formulas E = (O + 4M + P) / 6   —   σ = (P − O) / 6
aggregate σ = √(Σ σ²)   —   buffer = k · aggregate σ
Worked example · person-days
Item OMPEσ
A35125.81.5
B2353.20.5
C8122513.52.8
D1242.20.5
E58209.52.5
Total ——6634.24.1

Buffer at 2σ = 8.2 days, that is 24% of the expected estimate. Contractual commitment = 34.2 + 8.2 = 42.4 person-days, at roughly 98% statistical coverage. Naively summing worst cases would give 66 days: a 93% margin, which no client accepts and no serious supplier proposes.

Caveat · the assumption that can break

Root-sum-of-squares assumes independence between items. Correlated risks — same developer, same unclear requirement, same external dependency — violate that assumption and understate the buffer.

Inferred rule of thumb: where several items share one risk source, treat them as a single item before computing σ.

10Move 03 · early warning

The Buffer Is Meant to Burn — and to Be Watched

A buffer nobody monitors is not margin: it is a deferred surprise. Critical Chain's fever chart compares two percentages — how much work is complete and how much buffer has burned — and colours the result.

Thresholds and required action
Zone Condition Action
Green Buffer burned < half of progress None; carry on
Amber Between half of and equal to progress Prepare a recovery plan; do not execute it yet
Red Buffer burned > progress Execute the plan; tell the client now, not at deadline

The real value is not the chart: it is the lead time. Amber typically appears weeks before the deadline is at risk — early enough that the options still available are reprioritization and conversation, rather than raiding another client's capacity.

11Move 04 · upstream of all

Quote in Ranges, Not Numbers

Moves 01–03 protect the project after signature. This is the only one acting on the number that goes into the contract. If that number is fiction from the start, everything else is good engineering on a rotten foundation.

Four practice changes, by rising political cost
Practice Cost Effect
Never a scalar: always three points Zero Signals that uncertainty is structural, not incompetence
Label the cone stage in writing Minimal Turns uncertainty into a defensible artefact
Reference class forecasting from real history Data setup Replaces intuition with a comparable datum
Separate the sizing call from the price commitment Process change Breaks the anchoring → underestimation loop
Boilerplate for every initial quote This estimate sits at the “[approved product definition]” stage of the Cone of Uncertainty and therefore carries, by industry convention, a 0.5x–2x range against the final figure. A narrower range requires a discovery phase, quotable separately.

On reference class forecasting: humans are systematically optimistic estimating a project “from the inside”, and reasonably accurate comparing it to similar completed projects “from the outside”. The necessary condition is clean history — and here section 06 returns: if historical timings contain unrecorded overtime and capacity pulled from other clients, the outside view reproduces the distortion instead of correcting it.

12Move 05 · portfolio level

Make the Shared Pool Visible

The first four moves reduce the need to divert capacity between clients. This one makes the remainder visible, which is the only thing that lets future quotes be corrected rather than inherit the distortion.

Three metrics, by rising collection cost

Metric What it reveals
Planned hours per contract against actual hours per contract Direction and size of the transfer between clients
Total pool commitment against nominal capacity Structural over-commitment, distinct from poor execution
Per-team velocity variance, sprint over sprint The empirical input for move 02's buffer
Why this move is tagged untested

Moves 01–04 have literature and precedent behind them. This one is a recommendation derived from the reasoning above, not from a measured case: there is no internal baseline yet to compare results against. Treat it as an experiment with an outcome to verify, not as established practice.

13Sequence

Adoption Order

Ordered by rising political cost, not by importance. Every row is reversible and does not require the previous one to be complete — but the early ones produce the data the later ones need.

Horizon Action Sign-off from
Week 1 Internally written Change Request for every new requirement, with estimated delta, before it enters a sprint Nobody
Sprint 1–2 Three-point estimate on every new item; start collecting velocity variance The team
Month 1–2 Range and cone stage declared in every initial quote Whoever signs quotes
Quarter 1 Changes for Free clause in the SOW template; calculated buffer in every quote Management + counsel
Quarter 2 Per-contract fever chart; shared pool visibility; reference class from cleaned history Management

The week 1 row requires nobody's permission and is the one that builds the history all the others need. It is the recommended starting point.

14Close

The Discipline That Matters

None of the mechanisms described works unless it is decided explicitly, in writing and before work starts, which two of the three vertices stay fixed.

Everything else — clauses, buffers, fever charts, ranges — is machinery for making that choice honest and visible, instead of letting everyone politely lie to each other until the timeline explodes. The machinery is useful. But without the upstream choice, it has nothing to make honest.
