009 · Chapter I · Refinanced

The mechanism

Where the hurry comes from, how it travels down the chain, and why it turns faster at the bottom than at the top.

The tool is optional. The pace isn’t.

In 2026 the big cloud providers — the hyperscalers — will spend, according to the guidance the companies themselves gave in late July, between $720 and $745 billion on data centers, chips and energy. David Cahn, of Sequoia, has redone the math from the side of those who have to pay it back: justifying this year’s investment alone takes about $1.5 trillion in revenue from end customers. The industry doesn’t have that revenue yet.

This chapter is not about a tool. A tool can be picked up and put down, used where it helps and left where it doesn’t; we have our opinions on that, and they are in chapter II. Here we talk about the transmission: how a figure decided in a few boardrooms reaches our desks, and why it arrives accelerating.

I · 1The big wheel turns slowly

A large gear driving a small one makes it turn faster. The ratio is the ratio of the teeth: if the large one has seventy-two and the small one twelve, for every turn of the first the second makes six. It is the principle of every clock, and it is a surprisingly precise model of what is happening, because it explains something that looks irrational up close: at the top nobody is in a hurry, at the bottom everyone is.

  1. Capital. Multi-year investments, decided in a few boardrooms and depreciated over years. It turns slowly, but with enormous torque: when it moves, everything beneath it moves.
  2. Vendors. Those who sell models and tools have to turn that capital into recurring revenue. Out come price lists, plans, a new version every few months — and the story that comes with each one.
  3. Boards and CEOs. They read the story and translate it into targets: “AI-first”, shares of generated code to reach, sometimes hiring put on hold to see what the agents can do. The unit of measure is the quarter.
  4. Clients. They have read the same articles. They expect it to cost less and arrive sooner, and they say so at the negotiating table, on quotes measured in weeks.
  5. Developers. The smallest wheel. It receives all the motion accumulated above, at the pace of the working day: this morning’s ticket, Friday’s demo.

None of these stages is acting in bad faith. Each does the reasonable thing, given the motion it receives. And this is where mechanics says something uncomfortable: in a simple gear train, the final ratio depends only on the first wheel and the last. The ones in between don’t set the speed, they transmit it — and they turn in alternating directions, which gives each of them the impression of going its own way. A tech lead setting a deadline, a salesperson cutting a quote, ourselves when we promise a client a date: we believe we are choosing, and largely we are transmitting.

I · 2The fuel is perception

A gear doesn’t need to know why it turns: it is enough that the wheel above it moves. In this train, the wheel above moves on perceived gains more than on measured ones.

In 2025 METR had experienced developers work on their own open-source projects, randomly assigning the tasks where they could use AI and the ones where they couldn’t. At the end, participants estimated that AI had cut their time by about 20%. Measured, they had taken 19% longer.

It is a single study, and the 2026 follow-up is much less clear-cut: METR itself calls it “very weak evidence.” Other studies find real gains, especially for less experienced developers; the full picture, with what is measured and what is only forecast, is in chapter III. But to understand the mechanism you don’t need to know who is right about speed. You need to know that perception and measurement can sit on opposite sides of zero — and that the train is connected to the first.

The reason is mechanical too. Perception is fast: a demo lasts ten minutes, and it convinces. Measurement is slow: knowing whether a year of generated code holds up in maintenance takes a year, plus a way of measuring it that almost nobody has. A gear train transmits the motion there is, not the motion that would be right. And the motion there is, at every stage, is the impression of the stage above.

I · 3The teaser rate

The first year of coding assistants was sold flat-rate: a fixed fee each month, use as much as you like. In about fifteen months every flat plan went metered. Cursor in June 2025, with a public apology and refunds. Anthropic with weekly limits, in August 2025, after describing a single user who had consumed tens of thousands of dollars of model usage on a $200 subscription. Codex with credits, in April 2026. GitHub Copilot in June 2026, explaining that the previous model was no longer sustainable for agentic use.

Usage grew in the meantime. According to Jellyfish, which measures its own customers’ teams, the median user’s monthly spend went from $5 to $81 in ten months: sixteen times as much.

In finance this shape has a name: an introductory rate, or teaser rate. A mortgage with a few low-rate months up front is not a gift; it is how the customer gets in the door. The flat rate did exactly that job: it got us used to it, made the new pace normal, pushed quotes down. Then the rate turned variable — and the variable rate is set by the lender.

The price of a single token, meanwhile, is falling; the tokens an agent consumes per task are rising. Which of the two curves wins is a forecast, not a measurement: you will find both in chapter III. What is already visible is where the pressures meet, which is at the bottom of the train. The client expects it to cost less, having read that AI makes everything faster; the vendor charges more and more for exactly the heavy use that would be needed to make it cost less. In between sits the small wheel.

I · 4Deterministic machines, non-deterministic hands

The seduction of the compiler

The most convincing argument for no longer reading the code is a historical one. None of us reads the assembly that comes out of the compiler. People once did, out of distrust; then they stopped, and nobody misses it. Why should code written by an agent be any different? It will just be one more level of abstraction.

It is a trap, and it is worth saying exactly where it lies. The compiler earned that trust. It is deterministic: the same source produces the same binary, today and ten years from now. It is specified: there is a document that says what it must do, and when it doesn’t, that is a compiler bug — reproducible, reportable, fixed once and for all. An agent has none of these properties. The same prompt produces different code; there is no specification of what should come out; an error does not recur on demand. It is not a higher-level compiler. It is a very fast colleague, and should be treated as one.

The honest objection

The answer that always comes, and it is a fair one: we aren’t deterministic either. Two developers never write the same function the same way, and neither does the same developer a month apart. We have always built deter­ministic machines with non-deter­ministic hands.

That is exactly why, around the hand, we built a deterministic plant: types, compiler, tests, review, continuous integration. Every station exists to absorb the variability of whoever is writing. And the plant worked for four reasons we never named, because they came free.

  1. Variability was bounded by under­standing. Style varies from person to person; intent holds. Someone who has understood the problem gets the details wrong, not the direction.
  2. It was throttled by the fingers. We wrote more or less at the speed we thought, and verification could keep up. The governor was there: it was the keyboard.
  3. Human errors have tells. The code of someone who hasn’t understood gives itself away: it hesitates, contradicts itself, gets names wrong. A model’s text is uniformly fluent, and the right and the wrong wear the same face (we wrote about this in issue 003, “The map that isn’t there”).
  4. The cause could be reconstructed. Behind every line there was someone to ask why. git blame led to a person who might not remember, but had once had a reason. Today it leads to a session that no longer exists.

The disaster, then, is not non-determinism: we have lived with it for as long as the trade has existed. It is non-determinism at a rate higher than verification can absorb, with no one holding the theory.

The plant doesn’t break: it queues. Faros AI, which measures its own customers’ teams, found across 22,000 developers in the second quarter of 2026 more tasks completed, fewer deployments, and review times multiplied by five. It is vendor data, and a correlation; but it describes station 20 of the figure exactly. And when the queue is long, sooner or later someone opens the bypass: “looks right,” approved.

We have always built deterministic machines with non-deterministic hands. What’s new is that the hands no longer carry a head — and nothing slows them down.

Exploring and committing

Not all non-determinism is a defect. Upstream, while exploring, it is a virtue. Fred Brooks, in 1975, advised designing the first system knowing it would be thrown away; for the first time, throwing one away is cheap. Three prototypes in an afternoon, compared and discarded, are an honest use of a hand that never writes the same thing twice.

In maintenance the opposite holds. There, the same question needs the same answer, today’s change has to be consistent with the one from six months ago, and someone has to know why. The problem is not using the fast hand: it is using it at the same pace in both phases, because the train above makes no distinction. All it cares about is the turning.

I · 5From technical debt to cognitive debt

In 1985 Peter Naur published a short essay, Programming as Theory Building. Its thesis is simple and uncomfortable: a program is not its code. It is a theory — what it does, why it is built that way, how it can be changed without breaking it — and that theory lives in the heads of the people who built it. The code is a trace of it, always incomplete. When the team that holds the theory breaks up, for Naur the program dies, even if it keeps running: those who come after can modify it, but no longer know how to extend it without deforming it.

Technical debt we know: shortcuts taken in the code, paid back with interest on every later change. It has the merit of being visible; you just open the file. Cognitive debt is something else: code that exists without anyone holding its theory.

Technical debt you can see in the code; cognitive debt lives in the space between the code and the people.

And here is the paradox: code written by an agent is often cleaner than ours. Well-chosen names, short functions, tidy comments. That is exactly why the debt hides. The heuristics we use to spot problem code look at the surface, and the surface is impeccable.

That leaves the step that gives this issue its title. Agents make refactoring cheap: renaming, extracting, reorganizing a whole module takes minutes. Technical debt is therefore cheaper to carry, because you can always clean up later. But every cleanup is one more change nobody internalized, one more piece of theory that never made it into anyone’s head. We haven’t paid off the debt. We have refinanced it: it has moved from the code into the people, at a variable rate, and the rate is set by the lender.

The tool is optional. The pace isn’t.

We can choose when to use an agent, and where. We can’t choose how fast the top wheel turns, nor how much the ones in between multiply it. What is left to us is deciding where to fit the governor.

What it feels like to stand on the small wheel, and what we have decided to do about it, is chapter II.

Chapter II

Our reaction

What we feel, what we have done, and what it cost us to learn it.

Chapter I described a gear train that multiplies speed from the top down. This chapter is about the last wheel, the smallest and the fastest: us.

§ IThe unease is not the tool

Let us start from a measurement, because it is more honest than mood. A product we have been working on for a year, about two thousand commits, used to move at a few dozen commits a month. In July 2026, during a port, it logged 638, 147 of them in a single day. By September nearly all of them carried an agent’s signature as co-author.aSeptember 2026From the product’s git log, counting the signatures of all agents: 98% of September’s commits. The month-by-month count is in chapter III.

None of these numbers, on its own, makes us uneasy. A tool is something you pick up and put down: we use it where it fits, we leave it where it doesn’t, and we have never had doubts about that. The unease is something else, and it took us a while to give it a name.

The tool is optional. The pace isn’t.

What market pressure is taking from us is not the right to write code by hand. It is the choice of when. Until recently we decided, task by task, what was worth writing line by line and what could be generated, copied, delegated. Today that decision is more and more often made by the pace: the estimate the client expects, the deadline set to a productivity that someone perceived more than measured.bMETR, 2025Experienced developers, on their own repositories: convinced they were 20% faster, measured 19% slower. The industry’s other numbers are in chapter III. Writing by hand is becoming something you have to justify.

Then there is nostalgia, which has to be looked in the eye: it is easy to use it as an argument when it is only a feeling, and just as easy to dismiss it as a feeling when it is an argument.

Part of it is feeling, and that is legitimate. It is authorship: recognizing our own hand in a file, knowing that a slightly crooked but correct solution is ours. It is flow: the hours when the problem and the fingers move at the same speed and everything else disappears. We miss them. But no client has ever paid for them, and we can miss them without making them a principle.

The other part is functional, and here nostalgia is right. Writing by hand was slow, and the slowness was the channel through which the theory of the program, in Naur’s sense, which chapter I has already introduced, got into our heads. Every line typed was a small decision that could not be skipped. When a defect turned up we knew where to look, because we had already been there. Friction was not the price of understanding: it was how understanding happened. If we remove the friction, that channel has to be rebuilt somewhere else. Or we accept, knowingly, that we no longer hold the theory.

A comparison helps. When photography went digital it lost the darkroom: the baths, the smell, the hours in the dark. The photographers who stayed good did not save the darkroom. They saved the eye: knowing what you want before you shoot, and recognizing at once when you haven’t got it. For us the distinction is the same: keep the eye, leave the darkroom. The trouble is that today the pace presses on both alike.

§ IIWhat we did, and what it cost

The product above is where we learned almost everything, and almost always by paying for it. We tell the story without names, because the product isn’t ours; the dates, on the other hand, are real, and they sit in the margin. It is also where we saw refinancing up close: redoing things is cheap, so things get redone, and every tidy-up is one more change that nobody made their own.

The first lesson came on July 25. An agent-led cleanup deleted 68 style blocks “because nothing referenced them.” Five were live features, just not wired up yet.cJuly 25, 202668 blocks deleted, 5 of them live. The rule born that day: never use the port as the oracle for what the port should contain. In the same pass an unverified “this API is unused” turned into two deletions, a kill list, and an exclusion from the security matrix. “Nothing references it” was true of the code and false of the intent. The intent lived in a head, and that head was not in the room.

Over the same two days, agents working in the same folder destroyed each other’s work.dJuly 25–26, 2026One working tree, several agents, edits overwritten. Today whoever builds in parallel gets a worktree of their own and integrates by rebase. By August 6 the instructions file for the agents had reached 877 lines. That was the shape our lessons took: paragraphs of prose, for a reader who might follow them, or might not.

On August 28 we bought the same lesson twice, on the same day. Two agents that didn’t know about each other each paid for it separately: a test that passed just the same with the data leak present, because the field that could carry the leak never got that far.eAugust 28, 2026A vacuous absence assertion, discovered twice. Today it is an entry in our trap register, with its date. A lesson learned by a session that then closes is a lesson nobody has learned.

On September 3 we measured the comments. They made up 25.6% of the source, and 56% of the mass of the long blocks had been written by agents in the previous two weeks: not historical debt, a living habit. An earlier cleanup of 880 lines had been rewritten, within a month, at a ratio of twenty to one. The rule asking for short comments that say why held only once it became a test.fSeptember 3, 202625.6% comments; −880 lines rewritten 20:1 in a month. The test that stopped the wall runs in 0.44 seconds.

And in cleaning up we erred in the opposite direction. Compressing a wall of comments in place, instead of moving it elsewhere, throws away the receipts along with the reasoning: the short facts that were meant to stay (§ IV says which). The first time we lost about twenty-five. On October 2 it happened again.gOctober 2, 2026Of the 509 comment lines deleted, 248 were receipts: almost one in two was a fact, not an opinion.

Lined up, these stories have the same shape. In each one there was a piece of knowledge: in a head, in a file, in a session. And in each one a stochastic reader followed it, or didn’t. The problem was not a lack of rules. It was that our rules were prose.

§ IIIFrom prose to mechanism

From there came everything we built afterward, and it comes down to two shifts: from prose to mechanism, and from a single head to a shared place. Prose asks; a mechanism checks. A reader, human or model, can skip a paragraph. A failing test can’t be skipped.

Rules travel as tests.

The first step was odoo-template, the template our Odoo projects are born from, with the style rules collected in Code Craft, issue 006. There, some rules stopped being advice: a refactor is never mixed with a change in behavior, and every fix comes with a test that fails without it. The template itself also reaches the projects it generated through migration recipes with executable checks, rather than through a document to read.hOctober 1, 2026Twenty-seven recipes, the first one from August 28. Each can tell on its own whether it has already been applied.

The second is devkit, the team kit: everyone’s rules and agents, installed from a single source on every machine. Its central idea is blind review. The reviewer receives neither the plan, nor the rationale, nor the report of whoever wrote the code: it receives the diff. And the tests come after the review, because tests encode the rationale, and reading them would cost the reviewer its blindness.iAugust 28, 2026A second review pass, shown only the fixes, found four more confirmed defects, among them a success message that hid a lost approval.

We had already tried the same principle in August, as a declared experiment: Metaconcert, told in part V of Factory Settings, issue 005 (slides 17–22). Members isolated by a membrane each work on their own, then the results are compared without being made to converge, and a person reconciles the divergence. One rule above all: artifacts cross the membrane, reasoning doesn’t. It is the same rule that today keeps the reviewers of devkit and of Claustrum blind.jAugust 7, 2026An experimental protocol, declared “untested.” Its limit, put in writing: the membrane didn’t stop everything, and a stale state of the repository reached the members outside the prompt.

The third is Claustrum, which issue 008 covers in full. Here only one thing matters: the blindness that was a request in devkit is a constraint in Claustrum. The runner refuses a brief for a blind role that carries the plan or the rationale, and it measures what changed from git, not from the agent’s report. Its README gives the reason for the whole journey in one line: a discipline that belongs to one person is not a discipline.

It is not a new idea, and it is only fair to say so. It is the move we have always made: around a non-deterministic writer, build a deterministic chain, made of types, compiler, tests and review, that contains its variance. Chapter I explains what has changed. For us the practical difference comes down to one thing: the chain has to be sized for a pace we don’t set.

And here is the less comfortable part: rules accumulate debt of their own. The 877-line instructions file was made of rules. A notes file that had grown past three thousand lines was made of rules. And on October 2 we found a stale rule, the one that required permission for every commit, copied from the template into every project it had generated.kOctober 2, 2026A leftover, not a choice: no one had been deciding it anymore. Removed from the template that same day. We moved the debt from the code to the rules. It is still debt, and we pay it in attention: a rule in a file is code nobody rereads, and it needs the same care. A date, a test when possible, and the willingness to delete it.

§ IVWhat we keep

We have no conclusion that holds for everyone, and we distrust anyone who has one. We have a criterion, which we apply to ourselves.

By hand where knowing matters. Generated where having will do.

Where we need to hold the theory, that is, in the heart of the domain and in the code we will have to explain to a client or fix in the middle of the night, we write it ourselves, or at least we read it as if we had. There, friction is the work, not a cost to cut. Where having the result is enough, as with scaffolding, repetitive migrations and throwaway prototypes, we generate without remorse, and we say so.

The second thing we keep is a writing rule, and it applies to code as much as to this newsletter. Next to the work stays only what is short and would change along with it: a decision with its date, a measured number, a trap called by its name (“it isn’t dead code: it’s a feature the port hasn’t wired up yet”). These are the receipts. The rest — the reasoning that brought us there, the alternatives we discarded, the history — goes into a separate document, and next to the code stays a one-line pointer. A wall of explanation at the top of a method looks like knowledge put somewhere safe; in fact it is the first text to stop telling the truth when the code changes. This page is built the same way: the reasoning in the middle, and in the margin the dates and numbers it rests on, there to be checked.

The third is the choice of when. The pace tries to take it from us; we try to take it back before it is needed, deciding project by project which parts are there to be understood and which only to be had, before the deadline decides for us. We don’t always manage. When we don’t, at least we note it in the margin, with the date.

That is the story so far. Chapter III sets the numbers beside these dates: what we did, what was measured, what is forecast.
