Part I · The biases
01What they are

They aren't random errors. That is precisely the problem.

A bias isn't a slip. It's a shortcut the brain uses because most of the time it works: fast, nearly free, and wrong rarely enough to have survived.

The catch is that when it's wrong, it's wrong in the same direction every time.

Noise averages out across enough attempts. Bias doesn't: the more data you gather, the more precise you become about the wrong place.
established
Noise
Every shot imprecise, the average right. More trials → closer to the truth.
Bias
Every shot precise, all in the same wrong place. More trials → more confidence, same error.
Part I · The biases
02The blind spot

We spot them in other people effortlessly. Pointed inward, the mechanism doesn't fire.

Pronin, Lin and Ross (2002) asked people how susceptible they were to a list of biases relative to the average person. Almost everyone placed themselves below average. Including after each bias had been explained to them in detail.

It has a name — the bias blind spot — and it is itself a bias. It is among the most replicated results in the field.

Knowing about a bias doesn't switch it off. It's information about what you know, not about what you do.

03The four families

Two hundred names, four problems.

The list of documented biases is enormous and largely redundant. It's more useful to look at the four problems the brain is solving when it produces them. From here on, every bias carries its family's mark.

Too much information

We filter

We notice what confirms what we already think, what's recent, what's vivid. The rest never arrives at all.

confirmation · anchoring · availability
Not enough meaning

We fill the gaps

We build coherent stories out of incomplete data, then treat the story as though it were the data.

narrative fallacy · hindsight · curse of knowledge
Need to act fast

We hand ourselves confidence

We overestimate control and speed, and defend whatever we've already invested in. Useful for starting, not for being right.

overconfidence · planning fallacy · sunk cost
What to remember

We compress

Peaks, endings and survivors stay. The rest of the dataset disappears without notifying us.

peak–end · survivorship · availability

Part II · In software
05Where intuition calibrates

Intuition only learns where feedback is fast and not up for debate.

Kahneman and Klein, after years of disagreeing with each other, settled on one condition (2009): expert intuition is trustworthy only where the environment is regular and feedback is rapid and unequivocal. Miss either one and experience produces confidence, not accuracy.

experience corrects you experience just convinces you
Debugging beats bias out of you in seconds: you have a hypothesis, you run it, the machine says no. Architecture never corrects you — by the time a choice is visibly wrong the team has changed, the context has changed, and the failure is attributable to anything at all.

Part II · Lifecycle phases
06Design

The first proposal on the table doesn't get evaluated. It gets amended.

Anchoring

The first architecture proposed never goes head to head with the alternatives: it becomes the perimeter inside which alternatives are born. Which is why they show up as "variants of that one".

Availability

You pick the technology you used recently, or the one you just read something enthusiastic about. Not the one that fits: the one nearest the top of the mental stack.

Planning fallacy

You estimate from the inside, summing the parts. The result describes with great precision the scenario in which nothing unexpected happens — which is to say, no scenario that has ever occurred.

Narrative fallacy

A tidy explanation feels true. Real systems are messy: if the diagram is too clean, the likelier candidate isn't an elegant system — it's a missing piece.

How often has the second option been born before the first was already on the table?
Part II · Lifecycle phases
07Writing and debugging

Debugging is hunting for confirmation of a theory you formed in three seconds.

Confirmation bias

The first hypothesis arrives immediately, and from then on you go looking for the logs that support it. Wason's 2-4-6 task shows this is the default behaviour: people test cases that confirm the rule, not cases that would break it.

Curse of knowledge

Names and comments get written for someone who already holds the context in their head. That someone doesn't exist: it's you in six months, and you won't have it.

Fundamental attribution error

"Works on my machine." When the code is ours the cause sits in the environment; when it's someone else's the cause sits in the person. Identical bug, two different explanations.

Local optimization

You improve what you're looking at, because it's what you're looking at. The real bottleneck is rarely in the open file.

The question that breaks the loop is always the same: what observation would show my hypothesis is wrong, and what does it cost to get hold of it?
Part II · Lifecycle phases
08Maintenance and evolution

Nobody removes anything. Everybody adds.

Status quo bias + loss aversion

Adding a feature is a visible gain, removing one is a visible loss. Nobody gets thanked for a deletion. The system can only grow in one direction.

Sunk cost

The more hours are already in it, the harder it becomes to throw away. The correct question ignores the past entirely: from here forward, does rewriting cost less than continuing?

Normalization of deviance

Every tolerated exception shifts the line of normal by a fraction. No single step looks wrong, and the sum is unrecognisable. We covered this one with the broken windows.

Diane Vaughan, on the Challenger analysis
Hindsight bias

In the post-mortem the cause looks obvious. Looking obvious afterwards is exactly what makes the lesson useless: you learn "this time it was evident", not "here is what we were not able to see".

A useful post-mortem writes the timeline before the conclusion. If the conclusion comes first, the timeline just confirms it.
Part II · In software
09Every role, its bias

The higher you climb, the longer the feedback loop.

Junior
Fluency illusion. Reading clear code feels like understanding it. The feeling of comprehension and comprehension itself are two different quantities, and only one of them holds up in front of an incident at midnight.
minutes
Senior
Curse of knowledge. You cannot go back to not knowing. What's obvious to you doesn't get said — not in a code review, not in a prompt. It's the most expensive bias because it's invisible to whoever has it.
weeks
Lead / PM
Planning fallacy, escalation of commitment, halo effect. Inside-view estimates, projects defended past the point of no return, assessments of people contaminated by a single positive trait.
months
Owner / CEO
Survivorship, social proof, confirmation. Success stories are written by the survivors; the failures don't publish blog posts. And the metrics that prove a decision was right often get chosen after it was taken.
years · or never
It isn't that the people deciding are less clear-headed. It's that they receive fewer corrections, later, and in a more ambiguous form.

evidence boundary

Measured, replicated, with a known margin of error. You can rest the weight of a decision on it.

inferred

Derived from known mechanisms, coherent but not measured. Good for a reversible decision, not an irreversible one.

Plausible, no data. Counts as a working hypothesis and as something to go and measure, not as an argument.

Part III · With agents
11Friction is information

A colleague resists you. That's how you find out your premise was wrong.

Hand a bad premise to a person and you get confusion, a dumb clarifying question, an odd silence, a face. They aren't telling you the right answer: they're telling you something doesn't add up in your framing. Most of how a team self-corrects runs through exactly that.

Human colleague — high-friction channel
bad premise→person→partial answer, late
↩ "what do you mean?"↩ dumb question↩ hesitation↩ puzzled face
Model — near-zero frame friction
bad premise→model→same premise, better argued
no return signal · confidence rises · the next prompt is more committed than the last
Prior in, better-argued prior out. It isn't that the model inherits our biases — it's that it hands them back in a more convincing form than the one we gave it.
Part III · With agents
12The four channels

The same four families, now outbound from the keyboard.

We filter → Specification

You encode the diagnosis, not the symptom

"Fix the race condition in the pool" has already committed the agent to your hypothesis. Whether or not there is a race condition, it will find one: you asked for it.

We fill the gaps → Omission

The gap fills itself

What's obvious to you goes unsaid, in front of a recipient that cannot pull a puzzled face. The gap fills with the modal pattern from training. It fails silently: no error, just plausible wrongness.

We hand ourselves confidence → Evaluation

From operator to monitor

The role humans perform worst — supervising a system that is almost always right — handed the most convincing possible input: an artifact written better than you'd have written it.

We compress → Persistence

The frame cannot decay

We forget, and that's how bad framings die in a human team. In a context window your opening frame is re-read every single turn. Compression never arrives here.

The research behind the third channel — automation bias

Not a recent intuition about AI: it's a human-factors research thread running since the nineties, born in aviation and continued in medical diagnostics. Operational definition: using automated output as a heuristic replacement for vigilant information seeking, with two error types — commission (you act on an incorrect recommendation) and omission (you fail to notice the problem because the system didn't flag it).

The result that matters most here: in a study on flight crews, training aimed specifically at automation bias reduced commission errors but not omission errors. Here too, knowing isn't enough.

Mosier et al. 1997 — cockpit Skitka et al. 2000 — training Parasuraman & Riley 1997 e-prescribing 2017
Part III · With agents
13The only serious measurement we have

When someone measured instead of asking, the sign came out the other way.

What it is. METR is a non-profit research institute that evaluates AI systems. In July 2025 it published a randomised controlled trial — the design used for drugs — on the effect of AI tools on real development work.

How it ran. 16 experienced developers brought 246 real issues from their own repositories, projects they had worked on for five years on average. For each individual task a coin flip decided whether AI was allowed or forbidden. Developers screen-recorded, and time was measured rather than self-reported.

The three figures. How much they expected AI to speed them up beforehand; how much they believed they had been sped up after finishing; and how much the clock actually moved.

The interesting datum isn't the slowdown. It's that after doing the work, on the clock, they still believed they had gone faster: 39 points of gap, all of it in the flattering direction.
METR — write-up arXiv 2507.09089
One study, small sample, early-2025 tooling on large mature repositories. METR itself now labels it historical. It does not show that AI slows people down — it shows that your perception of your own productivity is not a measurement.
Part IV · Countermeasures
14Change the process

Teaching people about biases barely works. Changing the process works.

It's the uncomfortable finding of the debiasing literature, and it's also why this deck, on its own, is nearly useless. What moves outcomes is the structure of the decision, not the awareness of whoever is deciding.

Against anchoring

Independent estimates before discussion. In a meeting the first number spoken always wins. Written separately, then compared: the divergence is the information.

Against overconfidence

Pre-mortem. Not "what could go wrong", but: it's a year from now, the project failed, write down why. Imagining a past generates far more causes than imagining a future.

Against the planning fallacy

Outside view. Don't decompose the estimate. Find five comparable projects already delivered and look at how long they actually took, including the ones that went badly.

Against confirmation

Explicit falsification. Before starting: what observation would change my mind? If there's no answer, it isn't a diagnosis — it's a preference.

Against hindsight

Timeline before conclusion. In post-mortems, reconstruct what was known at each moment, and only then draw the line.

Against contagion

Fresh eyes, unaccompanied. Whoever reviews must not be handed the author's explanation first. The explanation is the contagion: it transfers the blind spot along with the context.

That last one is the same idea which, applied to agents, becomes the instrument in Part V.
Part IV · Countermeasures
15Symptom, not diagnosis
Two buttons — compare the versions
Same bug, same context, same model. The only change is what it's allowed to know about what we think.
  • Describe what you observe, not what you think it is. If you need the hypothesis, put it in a later turn and explicitly ask for it to be taken apart.
  • Ask for at least three hypotheses, ranked by what it costs to rule each one out, before any change.
  • Ask "what did I fail to specify, and what did you assume?" — it turns silent omission into a readable list.
  • Don't let the context that wrote the code review the code. Fresh context, no rationale: the artifact and the criteria, nothing else.
  • Acceptance criteria written first, by something that will never see the implementation.
  • The alarm bell: if the answer agrees with you and does it better than you'd have argued it yourself, that's the moment to doubt, not to proceed.
  • Re-read your first message at turn twenty. It's still in the room, and it's still deciding.

METACONCERT
On-demand agent workflow for Claude Code
8 members + 1 director · MIT licence · efficacy unmeasured
◆ Written by me — this part isn't a press review

Not a better prompt and not a smarter agent. It's a protocol that treats cognitive bias as an engineering problem rather than a personality trait: eight subagents, each denied something specific, coordinated by a director that is forbidden from forming a view.

It restores friction by construction between members, and by discipline at the director. That second guarantee is the weaker of the two, and it's named here rather than hidden.

github.com/orlodax/metaconcert
Where the name comes from

In Julian May's Galactic Milieu novels a metaconcert is a set of independently operant minds fused under a director.

In those books the coordinator is always the failure point: a corrupt director turns the whole apparatus into an amplifier for one mind's intent. Hence a director here that enforces protocol and is forbidden from having a view.

The mark: the four bias families arranged around an empty centre. The centre is the director, and it's empty on purpose.

Stop 1 · divergence

Your instinct — quarantined from intake — presented beside the diagnosis derived by a member that never saw it. The director does not choose. Asked to, it declines and restates the divergence.

Stop 2 · open questions

The spec ambiguities the oracle refused to resolve on its own. They land before anything is built: silently picking an interpretation is how a spec gap becomes a shipped bug.

Stop 3 · reconcile

The criteria written in advance, set beside the attack findings. The gaps between them are the finding, not a mess to tidy up before showing you.

1
Independent, then compare. Never converge. Agreement between members that shared a frame is an echo. Only agreement between members that could not see each other is evidence.
2
Artifacts cross the membrane, reasoning does not. Rationale is the vector by which one member's blind spot becomes everyone's.
3
The human reconciles. If the workflow ever hands you a tidy consensus, it has failed silently.
4
Verdicts trace to observations. "Who was right" is decided at falsify by a pre-registered test, never at the divergence stop by judgement. Without a falsify observation the ledger reads unresolved.
Empty phase → skipped, and the director must state which bias is therefore uncontrolled for that run. It may never substitute itself for a missing member, nor summarise what members produce: the prose summary is precisely where the fluency illusion re-enters.

18The membrane

The exclusion is mechanical — but only for the part the director composes.

A subagent gets a fresh conversational context, and what the parent puts in the prompt string is the parent's deliberate channel into it. The director builds that prompt from the member's receives list and nothing else: what isn't on the list wasn't something the member was asked to ignore — it never arrived.

19The first error it caught

It was in the README of the person who wrote it. And in an earlier version of these slides.

The earlier version said: a subagent's context starts fresh, and the only channel from parent to subagent is the prompt string.

That was false. What caught it was the project's own protocol, on its first run.

A subagent's context is conversationally fresh, not empty. Alongside your prompt sits an ambient layer — harness instructions, tool definitions, environment and project files. That layer is not suppressible, and should not be: it's what makes a member a capable agent rather than a bare completion.

Run 002 located the edge of the scope: a stale snapshot of repository state reached members outside the prompt string entirely, and outside the director's control.

The corrected formulation: exclusion is structural for the material the director composes and chooses to withhold; exclusion of everything else is not.
An instrument that has not yet shown it works nevertheless produced the hardest thing to get in this trade: a retraction of a claim by its own author, with the correction written into the README in place of the wrong sentence.
It's also the only way this deck could stay consistent with itself. A slide about how biases survive, that had quietly kept an author's convenience already known to be false, would have been the point demonstrated from the wrong side.

20The members

Each one is defined by what it is not allowed to know.

mc-observer intake
Separates observation from diagnosis in the report. It doesn't discard your causal claims — it quarantines them, so human-vs-independent divergence stays measurable. If it finds itself writing "because", it stops and deletes the clause.
never sees: nothing — it's the entry pointsonnet · very low
mc-cartographer map
Externalises the system model before any change, labelling every single item [verified] / [inferred] / [assumed]. The assumed list is the externalised curse-of-knowledge gap. It never proposes changes; it describes what exists.
never sees: the quarantinesonnet · low
mc-diagnostician hypothesise
Minimum three hypotheses, at least one locating the fault outside the component the symptom appears in, each with a named falsifier. Ranked by information-per-cost, not by likelihood. It produces hypotheses, never fixes.
never sees: quarantine, forecastsonnet · low
mc-historian hypothesise
Reference-class forecasting from actual git history: finds work with the same structural signature and reports what it really cost, rework tail included. Forbidden from inside-view decomposition — that method is the disease.
never sees: quarantine, hypothesis-rankinghaiku · very low
mc-oracle specify
Writes acceptance criteria from the specification only, blind to the diff, and must name the plausible wrong implementation. It refuses to resolve spec ambiguity silently: ambiguities are listed as open questions.
never sees: diff, build-report, deliberationsonnet · low
mc-falsify falsifynew
Executes one pre-registered test, blind to which outcome anyone wants. Reports the raw observation before interpretation, and never says "confirmed" — only which branch matched. Against p-hacking and outcome-driven reading.
never sees: who wants which resultsonnet · low
mc-builder build
Receives the decision, not the deliberation that produced it — inheriting a rationale means inheriting its blind spots. On a map/reality contradiction it halts instead of repairing the map and continuing.
never sees: deliberation, rejected hypotheses, quarantinesonnet · medium
mc-adversary attack
No rationale in context. It cannot return a clean bill of health without enumerating what it checked, the evidence each check passed on, and what it could not check. It verifies by execution, not by reading and feeling convinced.
never sees: deliberation, build-report, rationaleopus, different family · high
Director not a subagent
The skill running in your main session. Enforces the membrane, surfaces divergence unresolved, and never forms a view. No summarising, no merging, no choosing, no self-substitution for a missing member. Against: re-centralisation, consensus pressure, premature synthesis.
never sees: an opinion of its ownfree in dispatches · paid in context

21Five ceilings, and the number

Isolation gives members independent conversational context and nothing more.

1
Shared priors. Shared weights; isolation decorrelates context, never training.
partial lever · run the attack phase on a different model family
2
Shared ambient layer. An identical harness layer in every member: ~28,500 tokens against a 257-token prompt in run 001.
no lever
3
Harness-forwarded parent state. Run 002: a stale repository-state snapshot reached members outside receives and outside the director's control.
no lever
4
Member tool grants. Read/Grep/Glob reach quarantine-class material sitting anywhere in the working tree, tracked or not — one grep for an error string lands a member in the file the quarantine withheld.
lever between runs only
5
A director that holds everything. Withholding is mechanical once decided; the deciding is discipline, not structure.
no lever inside a run
Divergence rate: how often the independently derived diagnosis disagreed with the belief quarantined at intake — and how often the independent one was right when it did. Normally unobtainable: you type your instinct into the prompt and destroy the measurement before anything can be compared against it.

It must open a clean session. Work normally for an hour and then invoke the skill and the quarantine comes back empty: you already talked yourself through the diagnosis. And if you have no hunch, "I don't know" is a complete answer — don't manufacture one to feed the machine.

Invocation is selected on failure. The protocol is only reached for after the cheap loop has already failed, so every run is drawn from problems pre-selected for the human's instinct having been wrong. That inflates both the measured divergence rate and the member-win rate. The control arm corrects for it only partially.

▪Closing

We can't become less susceptible to bias.
We can build processes that don't depend on us being.

Debugging corrects us because the feedback is immediate and not up for debate. The rest of our work has no such property, and agents have taken it away even where we had it. The countermeasure isn't paying closer attention: it's deliberately designing where information is permitted to travel.

The goal isn't being right more often. It's noticing sooner.
