The scissor effect

AI doesn't lift everyone.
It widens the distance.

Those who already have the mental models use AI as an accelerator: they read, discard, correct. Those who haven't built them yet accumulate code that works and nobody understands — including whoever shipped it.

In some introductory courses, after the assistants arrived, failure rates went up, not down. The code handed in was better. The people were not.

Why it happens to sharp people too

It isn't laziness.
It's how our heads work.

Automation bias

If it comes out of a machine, it looks already checked

We trust an output more just because the source seems competent. The fluency it arrives with is not evidence of correctness.

Us: when AI answers in two seconds, we tend to skip the question “but is it true?”.
Illusion of understanding

Reading something clear feels like having understood it

Writing the code by hand forced us to understand it. That friction was the work, not the obstacle. Removing it removes the check as well.

Us: we know that it works. Far more rarely do we know why.
Deferred cost

The speed shows today, the bill arrives in six weeks

Successful demos circulate. Rollbacks and 2 a.m. bugs don't. We systematically see more positive signal than there is.

Us: we measure how much we ship, almost never how much comes back.
One rule, if we had to keep only one
Ask AI only for things we could write ourselves.

Not because writing them by hand is better — it's slower and we know it. But because it's the only way to notice when the answer is wrong. If we don't know what a right answer looks like, we have no yardstick for recognizing a wrong one: we just take it.

Corollary, for prototypes: if it's a demo, anything goes. The line isn't “AI yes / AI no”. The line is production.

Three habits, zero bureaucracy

What we'll try
from the next sprint.

Before asking

Ten lines by hand

A rough draft of the logic we think is needed. Ugly is fine. Its only job is to give us an opinion before we hear the AI's.

In the PR

Two lines of “why”

Not what the code does — that part is readable. Why this solution, and what it doesn't cover. Written by us, not generated.

In review

Talk us through it

Data flow and edge cases, in one minute. If we can't explain it, it isn't ready — and that goes for everyone, not just the juniors.

None of these slow down anyone who has already understood the code. They only slow down whoever hasn't — which is exactly the point.

Not to police us. To know whether it's working.

Three numbers that tell
the truth about last month.

Rollbacks and hotfixes

How many times we had to go back. If it climbs while velocity climbs, we aren't going faster: we're borrowing.

Time to understand a bug

Not to fix it — to understand it. This is where code nobody really owns shows itself.

PRs merged without explanation

The share of code that reached production which nobody could (or had to) talk through.

Today we only watch half the cycle: how much goes out. These three numbers watch the other half: how much comes back. If they don't get worse after two sprints, we have proof we're using AI well — and we can push harder.

To take home

The risk isn't AI.
It's shipping something none of us understands.

The question, at the next PR

“If this broke in production at 11 p.m. tomorrow, and AI didn't answer — would I know where to start?”

If the answer is yes: let's use it as much as we like, genuinely. If it's no, it isn't a tools problem. It's a missing piece — and it's worth going to get it now, while it's cheap.
