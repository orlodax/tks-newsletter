1969 · The experiment

It starts with two abandoned cars, and it doesn't end the way the story is usually told

In 1969 the psychologist Philip Zimbardo left two identical cars, with no license plates and their hoods up: one in a Bronx neighborhood, the other in Palo Alto, California.

The Bronx one was set upon within minutes. The first to arrive were a father, a mother and a child, who stripped out the radiator and the battery. Within twenty-four hours nothing useful was left; then the windows, the seats, and kids playing inside it.

The Palo Alto one stayed untouched for more than a week. Then Zimbardo went there himself and hit it with a sledgehammer. At that point the others joined in too. In fairness it should be said that the car had meanwhile been moved onto the Stanford campus, and the first to take part were his own students.

The detail that stands out most is a different one: in both cases the majority of the adult destroyers were well-dressed, respectable-looking people. They were not bad people who happened to be passing. They were ordinary people, in a place where it looked as though nobody cared.

Zimbardo 1969 · Wilson & Kelling, The Atlantic 1982 · Bench Ansfield

How it became a theory

In 1982 Wilson and Kelling built their thesis on this experiment — which was also the only evidence they cited. And they simplified it considerably. Zimbardo was talking about inequality and anonymity; from it they drew the formula whereby one broken window produces a thousand.

That is the version everyone remembers, and it is the less interesting of the two. The real thesis was another: that visible disorder drives up serious crime. That is the one that was put to the test, and the one that has been argued over for forty years.

What survived

It wasn't the windows. It was whether anyone is watching.

Lowell, Massachusetts, 2005. Researchers identify 34 hot spots and treat only half of them: they collect the trash, repair the streetlights, enforce the building codes, add services for homeless people. And they also make more arrests for minor offenses.

In the treated areas, calls to the police drop by 20%. But the study's conclusion is the part that concerns us: what made the difference was fixing the environment, not the arrests.

A 2015 review of all the available studies says the same thing more bluntly. Acting on places works. Going after badly behaved people one by one produces no significant results.

Fixing places works
Going after people doesn't work

Lowell / Harvard & Suffolk 2005 · Meta-analisi 2015 · Sampson & Raudenbush · Harcourt & Ludwig 2006

The name for it

Sampson and Raudenbush showed that the direct link between visible disorder and crime is weak. What explains both is a third element, which they call collective efficacy: how connected the people of a place are to one another, and how far they take it for granted that the shared space gets looked after.

An almost cruel confirmation: when a public program moved families out of run-down neighborhoods into orderly ones, those families went on committing offenses at the same rate as before. Changing the windows around a person does not change the person.

The windows were not the cause. They were how you could see there was no longer anyone taking care of the place.
In code

In neighborhoods the link is weak. In code it is strong.

In neighborhoods the step from disorder to serious trouble is uncertain, because it has to pass through people's heads. In code it doesn't: it passes through the very way the code gets written. It happens in four very concrete ways.

The alarms stop working

With 400 warnings nobody reads the 401st. If a test fails at random, red no longer means “it's broken”. Here the escalation is plain: you lose the ability to see the serious fault.

The Challenger case

The sociologist Diane Vaughan reconstructed what had happened at NASA before the disaster. Every time a known defect failed to cause an accident, that defect became a little more normal. Nobody ever decided to be reckless: the line of what seemed acceptable simply moved, one small piece at a time. She calls it normalization of deviance.

Careful not to overdo it

“Broken window” makes you think of things you can see: names, indentation, formatting. But the serious problems usually cannot be seen. And there is a finding worth knowing: where the police cracked down on minor offenses, residents felt less safe, not more. A review that turns into a blanket inspection has exactly the same effect. And some dirty pieces are perfectly fine left dirty: prototypes, modules we are about to throw away.

What seems to work

Three ways to stop it. None of the three asks people to pay more attention.

Ownership
On the left there's a name. On the right there isn't.

What belongs to everyone belongs to no one

Back in 1972 the urban planner Oscar Newman had already set out the point: people take care of the spaces they feel involved in. An area becomes safer when the people living in it feel it is theirs too — not when it is more closely policed.

The same thing happens in code. The rot is almost never in the module with a clear owner: it is in the shared utilities, the configuration, the build, the integration tests. Areas everyone passes through and nobody lives in.

The promptThe folders nobody would call “mine” are almost always the ones where the decay starts. It is worth knowing which they are, before even deciding what to do about them.
Boundaries
Spread everywhere
Concentrated

Disorder isn't eliminated, it's concentrated

No city has ever managed to make disorder vanish completely. The ones that cope best keep it in specific areas, so that everything else stays readable.

Four hundred mediocre files are worse than one horrible file and three hundred and ninety-nine clean ones. In the second case you always know where you are; in the first you never do.

The promptMaybe the worst piece we all know about doesn't need fixing: it just needs fencing off, and saying so out loud. The same goes for flaky tests — quarantining them is a different thing from deleting or ignoring them.
Attention
How much you see, since you arrived

People who have been here a long time stop seeing anything

It isn't laziness, it's habituation: after a few months certain things simply stop reaching the eye. Someone arriving from outside, on the other hand, sees them all — but only for a short while.

There is also a recognition problem: shipping a new feature gets noticed, deleting dead code does not.

The promptA newcomer's first weeks are the only moment when someone sees the project as it really is. That information expires fast, and we usually let it expire.
The point

“They care” isn't a quality of people. It's collective efficacy, seen up close.

None of the three proposals asks anyone to try harder. That is not an accident.

The very same developer works carefully on one project and fast and badly on another. If it were a matter of character, that would not happen.

Jane Jacobs called it “eyes on the street”: a neighborhood's order is not held by the police, it is held by the shopkeepers standing in their doorways and the people looking out. In code it is the eyes that land on a file that isn't yours — and that at some point stop landing.

In neighborhoods the people who keep order are not the most civic-minded: they are the ones who have lived there a long time and will stay. If you take a shortcut today, you are the one who meets the problem tomorrow.

How it breaks

If you remove one of the three, care doesn't simply disappear: it turns into frustration. Someone who cares a great deal but cannot act sees every problem and can touch none of them. Sooner or later they leave — and the ones left are those who have stopped noticing.

Over time the team degrades on its own. And it looks like a people problem, when it is a problem of how the work is organized.

Permanence

On this service, two years from now, you will still be here.

Room to act

You can fix it right away, without opening a ticket and waiting for three approvals.

Clarity

You understand enough about how it is built to feel responsible for it.

These are things you observe, not judgments about people

2026 · What changes now

AI agents read our code and imitate it. At machine speed.

Of all the mechanisms above, copying was the slowest: it moved at the speed of how much code one person can write in a day.

An agent has no taste, does not know why that line is there, and feels no embarrassment. It looks at the surrounding code, works out “this is how it's done here”, and produces more of the same. Across dozens of files, in parallel, faster than we can reread it.

And escalation comes back, not repetition: if the surrounding code says the tests can be skipped, the agent does not add a bit of ugliness. It skips the tests, as naturally as it picks the name of a variable.

There is a second effect too, quieter: an agent adds houses to the neighborhood, but adds nobody to look out of the windows. The code grows; the eyes watching it do not.

Writing the rules in an AGENTS.md file helps, but it is an attempt to contradict what the code is already saying on its own. And the code is much bigger.

AGENTS.md ~200 lines of rules
The code ~400,000 lines of examples

We are not writing the instructions for the agents. We are writing the place they will live in.

Same propagation. All at once.
Prompts

Four questions to carry around, not four targets

None of these has a right answer. They are for looking at your own project with the eye of someone who has just arrived.

Ownership

If I open a folder at random, do I know who takes care of it?

The areas with no name next to them are almost always the ones where decay accumulates first. Knowing which they are is already a result.

Boundaries

Does the ugly piece we all know about have a boundary, or has it dissolved into the rest?

An ugly but contained piece costs far less than a little ugliness spread everywhere. It doesn't necessarily need fixing: perhaps it just needs bounding.

Attention

What did the last person to arrive notice?

They were probably right, and by now they probably don't notice it either. It is information that expires fast and that almost nobody collects in time.

Visibility

Around here, does it show when someone repairs something?

If the answer is no, the rest follows on its own — not because goodwill is lacking, but because invisible work always loses to visible work.

A caution, if you decide to measure

Every number, the moment you look at it, becomes a target — and from then on it measures less and less. A count can help make maintenance work visible; the moment it lands in an individual performance review, it stops saying anything useful.
